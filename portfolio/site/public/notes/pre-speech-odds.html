Pre-Speech Odds vs. Reality

For fifteen months, Kalshi has run betting markets on what Donald Trump will say in his speeches — will he say "Biden"? "Tariff"? "Hormuz"? We pulled every price, every trade, and every transcript to answer two questions: does anyone know what he'll say before he says it, and where does the betting crowd get it wrong? Short answers: we found no sign of insiders — but the crowd has habits you can see from orbit.

159 speeches, Apr 2025 – Aug 2026 3,896 phrase contracts 974,533 individual trades 58,960 settled contracts backing the track records
01

How we know when a speech started

Kalshi doesn't record when a speech actually begins — but the market tells you anyway. The moment Trump says a tracked word, that word's contract becomes settled fact (you can't un-say "Biden"), so its price slams to 99¢ and stays there. Below is the "Will he say Biden?" contract on August 3: at 18:03 the price jumps from 63¢ to 99¢ in a single minute. That jump is the word being spoken. We matched jumps like this against the word-by-word timestamps in the Factba.se transcript of the same speech, which pins down the true start — 19 minutes before the first tracked word landed. Everything left of the dashed line is betting that happened before anyone had heard a word. That's the only part of the tape this report trusts.

02

Are the prices honest?

A price in these markets is a probability: a 70¢ contract is a claim that there's a 70% chance he says the word. The honesty test is simple — gather every contract priced around 70¢ and check whether about 70% of them came true. Do that at every price level and you get the curve below; an honest market sits on the dashed diagonal. We drew it twice: once using our careful transcript-based cutoff, and once with a sloppier cutoff that lets in bets placed while the speech was already underway.

Both versions agree on the main flaw: contracts priced near a coin flip (40–60¢) came true meaningfully less often than their price claimed — the crowd pays a premium for "will he say it" whenever it feels like a toss-up. Cheap contracts and near-certain ones were priced roughly right. Notice also that the sloppy cutoff's suspiciously perfect top-right corner softens under the honest one: some of what looked like foresight was just people betting on words they were hearing live.

03

The market ignores its own history

Every phrase has a track record — across 58,960 settled contracts, we know exactly how often Trump says "tariff" or "fake news." So take each contract and ask: is its price above or below the phrase's lifetime rate? If prices already absorbed that history, every group below would sit near zero. They don't.

How each group actually did, relative to its price

Bars to the right: contracts that beat their price. Bars to the left: contracts that fell short.

Contracts priced far below the phrase's track record beat their price by 12–17 points; contracts priced far above it fell short by 7–17. In plain terms: when the price and the history disagree, bet on the history. The crowd trades the day's narrative and under-weights how often he actually says things.

05

A tempting story that didn't survive testing

Why do phrases get mispriced? A tidy theory: his vocabulary changes and prices lag behind. Each row below shows a phrase's long-run rate (hollow dot) drifting to its recent rate (solid dot), with the market's average price as the orange tick. Measured the obvious way, mispricing lines up with this drift impressively well.

Old rate → recent rate, vs. where the market priced it

Hollow dot = long-run rate · solid dot = recent rate · orange tick = average market price

We tested it properly, and it failed. The obvious measurement partly grades its own homework — the "recent" window overlaps the very events the mispricing is measured on. When we separated them cleanly (drift measured on spring events, mispricing measured on summer events), the relationship vanished — though only 18 phrases qualified for the clean test, so it's a weak instrument. Honest verdict: unproven. The better-built version of the "market is slow to update" idea is the next section, which uses only information available before each event.

06

Streaks: the question that cracked it open

If he's said a word at his last few events, is he more likely to say it at the next one?

At first glance, obviously yes: after three straight mentions, a phrase hits 62% of the time; after three straight misses, 25%. But that's mostly an illusion of composition — words on hot streaks are words he says all the time anyway. Compare each phrase to its own track record and the streak of hits tells you nothing new. A coin that landed heads three times isn't extra likely to land heads again.

A miss is different — for Trump. His vocabulary lives in eras: topics arrive, dominate every speech, then get dropped. When "ceasefire" went from every-speech to skipped-one-speech, that silence was real news — the word was leaving the rotation for good. In the numbers: after a fresh miss, a phrase comes up about 9 points less often than its track record predicts. His misses carry information. His streaks don't.

How the market handles streaks (3,149 priced contracts)

Each bar: how the group actually did, relative to its price. The market prices hot streaks almost perfectly — and won't believe a word has been dropped.

The market half-understands this. Contracts on hot-streak phrases were priced within a point of reality. But it keeps paying for words he's stopped saying: contracts on phrases missed three or more times in a row were priced around 37¢ and came true 27% of the time — paying 37 cents for something worth 27.

Is that gap money? Nominally, yes. Buying NO on every three-plus-miss phrase at real pre-speech prices — after Kalshi's ~7% fee and up to 2¢ of spread — would have returned 4–6¢ per contract, roughly 6–10% per speech-day, across the 168 chances the modern era offered. (Reassuringly, the same strategy applied to every contract nets zero after fees — the edge is specific, not an accounting artifact.) But 168 chances is a small book: one unlucky month erases it, and the next section shows this market gets sharper every quarter. A documented bias, not an ATM.

Half of this is Trump; half is human nature. Kalshi runs identical markets on other people — CEOs on earnings calls, press briefings. Two findings. First, the miss-signal is Trump-only: for everyone else, skipping a word one time says nothing about next time (14,111 cases, dead zero). A CEO who skips "AI" one quarter is right back at his usual rate the next — most speakers' vocabularies are stationary; Trump's has regimes. Second, the twist: the betting mistake is universal. On every subject, contracts on a just-missed word were overpriced by about 7 points (25,121 contracts checked against day-before prices). Everywhere, bettors make the same hope-trade — he didn't say it last time, surely this time — like betting extra on a slumping hitter because he's "due." On Trump it simply costs more — 8½ points instead of 7 — because his misses actually mean something.

Where he speaks matters enormously. At Cabinet meetings, tracked words hit 61% of the time; on scripted tele-rallies, 29%. A two-ingredient recipe — the phrase's track record plus the type of event — gets you most of the way to the market's own accuracy. And the crowd misjudges the scripted formats specifically: it overpays at tele-rallies by 12 points and rallies by 9, while underpaying bilateral meetings — the format where he wanders furthest off script — by 9.

Two more habits show up in the fine-grained data. Overnight excitement overshoots: contracts that jumped 15+ points between the previous day's close and the pre-speech morning came true 11 points less often than their new, higher price claimed; overnight crashes overshot the other way. And the trade tape shows who's wrong: where NO buyers had been active before a speech, prices were almost exactly right; where YES buyers ran unopposed, prices sat about 11 points too high. In these markets, aggressive optimism doesn't discover the truth — it inflates the price until a skeptic shows up.

07

Fifteen months of history: the market learned

Kalshi deletes minute-level data after about ten weeks, but daily summary files survive — so 121 older speeches, back to April 2025, join the sample through a coarser snapshot: the price range on the day before each speech. That snapshot is imperfect (we checked it against exact prices on the 648 modern contracts where both exist — correlated, but noisy, and biased toward 50¢), so the comparison below applies the same imperfect snapshot to both eras. Whatever it distorts, it distorts equally. The gap between the lines is real.

The 2025 crowd massively overpaid for longshots: words priced 35–50¢ the day before a speech came true just 18% of the time. Today the same price range misses by single digits. Whether traders got smarter, market makers got tighter, or Kalshi's phrase menus got more realistic, the market of April 2025 and the market of today are different animals — and any "strategy" backtested on the old days would not survive in the new ones.

08

The insider question

The hypothesis that started this project: a phrase with a suspiciously high price on suspiciously heavy betting means somebody knows. Our screen for that fingerprint — price above 60¢, at least twice the phrase's usual share of the event's betting, priced far above its track record — flagged 18 contracts in the modern era, and 16 of them hit. Enticing. So we ran the same screen on all fifteen months.

At full scale, the fingerprint evaporated. The historical era's 74 suspects actually did worse than their prices claimed. Across all 92 suspect contracts, hits came in at 78% against prices claiming 79% — the "insider" signature paid exactly nothing, and the modern 16-of-18 looks like what it probably was: a lucky streak in a small sample. The one screen that stays mildly positive — heavy betting on already-expensive contracts — doesn't need insiders to explain it, because expensive contracts ran slightly cheap across the board in both eras. And the list of suspects that missed reads like a highlight reel of hype: HORMUZ during a strait crisis, AFFORDABLE three times during affordability news cycles, PUTIN, TARIFF. The honest residual: our day-before snapshot can't see a same-day insider on the 946 markets that only opened on speech day. On everything we can see, the crowd isn't hiding insiders. It's just a crowd.
